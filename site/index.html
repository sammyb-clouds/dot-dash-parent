What a Dot Dash is

A small, hand-built object with a tiny screen, two arcade buttons, a buzzer and an LED. You tap a message out in Morse Code, or pick one of your own ready-made phrases, and it arrives on another Dot Dash, or in the Dot Dash app on a phone.

It is a messaging device and nothing else. That is the whole idea, and it is easier to show as a list of things it hasn't got:

Nocamera
Noweb browser
Nolocation tracking

There is nothing to scroll and nobody new to meet. A Dot Dash can reach up to twenty contacts, and every one of them is a contact you approved yourself.

How a message gets there

  1. Tap it out.

    Tap SELECT for each mark — a short tap is a dot, a longer press is a dash. The device learns your own rhythm, so a slow tapper and a fast one are both read correctly. Hold ENTER for about a second to send.

  2. It travels over Wi-Fi.

    There is no SIM and no mobile data. A Dot Dash remembers up to five networks and uses whichever it finds, so it works at home, at a grandparent's and on holiday without being set up again. Messages go through Dot Dash's own servers, not anybody else's.

  3. It arrives.

    On another Dot Dash, where it can play out mark by mark like a telegraph, or in the Dot Dash app on a phone. Someone with neither can be sent a private web link and reply from that.

Why Morse, and not a keyboard

Morse is a real encoding: a whole alphabet built out of two symbols and the gaps between them. That is the same idea underneath every digital system a child will ever use, and here it is small enough to hold in your head and see working.

It is also rare in being finishable. A few weeks of practice and you have it for life, and the practice is the messaging — every message sent is a rep. The device teaches it from nothing: Train adapts to how fast you actually tap rather than demanding textbook timing, and most people send their first messages with the ready-made phrases and pick up the code alongside.

And the slowness is the point. Morse is too slow to argue in and far too slow to doomscroll. What it is good at is the short, deliberate message you meant to send.

For the grown-up setting it up

  • You are the account. Each Dot Dash is paired to your account in the Dot Dash app, and you approve every contact by call sign. There is no discovery and no friend suggestions — a stranger's attempt becomes a request for you, not a message on the device.
  • You can read everything. Both sides of every conversation appear in the Log. If you have set a device up for somebody else to carry, it is worth telling them that plainly.
  • It never leaves the list. No camera, no microphone, no browser, no location — so there is no way to reach the open internet from a Dot Dash, intentionally or otherwise.
  • Someone without a device can still reply. A grandparent can be sent a private web link and message from any phone, with no app to install.
  • Fifteen minutes to set up the first one. Wi-Fi, an account, a six-character pairing code, and a call sign for the device. The FAQ walks through all five steps.

SMS notifications (optional)

Someone without a Dot Dash, like a grandparent, can message one from our chat page using a private link the account holder sends them. On that page they can choose to get an SMS notification when a new message is waiting. Notifications only say that a message is waiting and link back to the chat. They never contain what anyone wrote. This is optional. Nobody has to receive SMS messages to use Dot Dash.
